## 1. Ethiopia: Tigray goes dark as war returns
- **Tier:** 1 · CRITICAL
- **Tone:** grave, calm, restrained
- **Length:** about 85 s
- **Voice/music:** deep voice at 0.9×; low drone
- **On-screen title:** ETHIOPIA'S WAR IS BACK
- **Pronunciation:** Tigray = TIG-ray · Mekelle = meh-KEL-leh · Axum = AK-soom · Alamata = ah-lah-MAH-tah · Abiy = AH-bee
- **Sources:** Reuters via US News (25 Sept), AP (25 Sept), Al Jazeera (24 Sept), CNN (25 Sept), The National (AU estimate), Wikipedia: 2026 Ethiopian offensive

<<<
In northern Ethiopia, the phones have gone silent again.

Ethiopia is in the Horn of Africa. Its northern region, Tigray, lived through a war from 2020 to 2022 that may have killed up to 600,000 people, according to the African Union's mediator. A peace deal signed in Pretoria, South Africa, in November 2022 ended the fighting.

This week, that peace collapsed.

On the twentieth of September, seven armed groups from across Ethiopia formed an alliance to overthrow Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. Tigray's forces are among them. So is an Amhara militia that fought against Tigray in the last war.

On the twenty-second, local authorities say government drone strikes hit two schools in the town of Alamata, killing at least 14 people.

On the twenty-third, Tigrayan forces seized the airports at Mekelle, Axum and Shire. All flights to the region were cancelled.

Then, on Thursday night, phone and internet networks across Tigray went down. Each side blames the other.

Roads have closed. People are fleeing into the neighbouring Afar region.

The African Union has called for an immediate end to the fighting. The fear now is that neighbouring Eritrea is drawn in.

The last time Tigray went dark, famine followed.

## 2. Sudan: army chief shut out of the UN as the war grinds on
- **Tier:** 1 · CRITICAL
- **Tone:** grave, clear, balanced
- **Length:** about 80 s
- **Voice/music:** calm voice at 0.9×; low drone
- **On-screen title:** SUDAN'S LEADER KEPT OUT OF THE UN
- **Pronunciation:** Burhan = boor-HAHN · El Obeid = el oh-BAYD · Kordofan = kor-doh-FAHN · Boulos = BOO-lohs
- **Sources:** Sudan Tribune; Al Jazeera (24 Sept); The National (22 Sept); Middle East Monitor (25 Sept); Al Jazeera (6 Jul, El Obeid)

<<<
Sudan's army chief was expected to address the world at the United Nations. Instead, he is stuck at home.

Sudan, in north-east Africa, has been at war since April 2023. On one side is the national army, led by General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan. On the other side is a powerful paramilitary group called the Rapid Support Forces, or RSF. The war has forced millions of people from their homes.

This week, the United States did not issue General Burhan a visa to attend the UN General Assembly in New York.

According to a Sudanese diplomatic source and a Western analyst, the visa was tied to a condition: that Burhan sign a 90-day ceasefire proposed by the American envoy Massad Boulos. Burhan reportedly refused.

Sudan says barring a head of state breaks the agreement that hosts the UN in New York. UN Secretary-General António Guterres has expressed regret and called for the war to end.

Meanwhile, the fighting goes on. In the city of El Obeid, in the Kordofan region, around half a million people have been trapped as the RSF tries to take control of the surrounding roads.

The question now is whether pressure from Washington brings a ceasefire closer, or pushes it further away.

## 3. Mali: the army's worst defeat in years
- **Tier:** 1 · CRITICAL
- **Tone:** grave, restrained
- **Length:** about 85 s
- **Voice/music:** deep voice at 0.9×; low drone
- **On-screen title:** MALI: 100+ SOLDIERS KILLED IN ONE ATTACK
- **Pronunciation:** Dioura = dee-OO-rah · JNIM = JAY-nim · Goïta = go-EE-tah · Bamako = BAH-mah-koh
- **Sources:** Reuters via US News (21 Sept), AP (19 Sept), Africa Center for Strategic Studies, Wikipedia: Mali fuel blockade, HRW (21 Sept)

<<<
More than 100 soldiers killed in a single attack. And in Mali, saying so on television can send you to prison.

Mali is in West Africa's Sahel, the dry belt south of the Sahara. It has been fighting armed groups since 2012.

On the tenth of September, fighters from JNIM, a group linked to al-Qaeda, overran an army camp at Dioura, in central Mali. A security source told Reuters 130 soldiers were killed and more than 100 were taken hostage. JNIM claims it killed 150. The government has not released a figure.

The same group has spent a year blockading fuel into the capital, Bamako. Mali has no coastline, so almost all of its fuel arrives by truck. More than 300 tankers have been burned. The army now escorts fuel convoys into the city.

Mali's military government, led by General Assimi Goïta, promised to restore security. But the Africa Center for Strategic Studies says deaths linked to militant groups in Mali have tripled since the military took power.

And this month, a journalist was sentenced to two years in prison, after saying on television that JNIM controlled a city in the north.

What to watch now: the fate of the captured soldiers, and whether the fuel convoys keep getting through.

## 4. DR Congo: Ebola, first signs of hope
- **Tier:** 2 · SERIOUS
- **Tone:** measured, cautiously hopeful
- **Length:** about 80 s
- **Voice/music:** calm voice at 0.95×; soft, sombre bed
- **On-screen title:** EBOLA: THE FIRST SIGNS OF HOPE
- **Pronunciation:** Ituri = ee-TOO-ree · Kivu = KEE-voo · Bundibugyo = boon-dee-BOOG-yoh · Haut-Uélé = oh-weh-LEH
- **Sources:** UN News (23 Sept); WHO Disease Outbreak News; U.S. Embassy Nairobi (25 Sept); US State Department pledge via briefing sources

<<<
For the first time in months, health workers in Congo are seeing signs that Ebola can be pushed back.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo, in central Africa, is facing its second-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded. As of the twenty-second of September, there were 7,773 confirmed cases and 3,759 deaths.

Ebola is a severe virus that spreads through contact with the body fluids of sick people. This outbreak is caused by a strain called Bundibugyo. There is no approved vaccine or treatment for it yet.

Now, the World Health Organization says it is seeing the first encouraging signs of progress. Over the last three weeks, new cases fell by about 26 percent in Ituri province, the centre of the outbreak, and by about 15 percent in Haut-Uélé.

But in North Kivu province, cases rose by 73 percent over the same period. It is a region already scarred by conflict, which makes tracing and treating patients much harder.

The United States has pledged 267 million dollars to the response. And next door in Kenya, the US Embassy has warned that some travellers' Ebola screening records may have been exposed in a data breach.

The next few weeks will show whether the progress spreads, or whether North Kivu becomes the new front line.

## 5. The Sahel's deadly heat: super El Niño
- **Tier:** 2 · SERIOUS
- **Tone:** measured, urgent, clear
- **Length:** about 75 s
- **Voice/music:** calm voice at 0.95×; soft tension bed
- **On-screen title:** THE HEAT THAT COULD KILL 31,000 NIGERIANS
- **Pronunciation:** El Niño = el NEEN-yoh · Sahel = sah-HEL
- **Sources:** University of Chicago Climate Impact Lab study via CNN (23 Sept) and Phys.org; The Conversation

<<<
The deadliest weather event of the coming months may not be a storm. It may be heat.

Every few years, the Pacific Ocean warms in a pattern called El Niño, which shifts weather around the world. This year's is expected to be unusually strong, a so-called super El Niño.

A new study from the University of Chicago's Climate Impact Lab estimates it could cause around 451,000 extra heat-related deaths worldwide between now and February 2027.

And the country projected to suffer the most is Nigeria, with about 31,400 deaths.

The next three are also in Africa's Sahel, the dry belt south of the Sahara. Sudan, with about 17,500. Niger, about 10,000. And Chad, about 8,000.

These are estimates, not certainties. But they point to a hard truth. The countries that have done the least to heat the planet are among those expected to pay the highest price.

Researchers say much of this is preventable. Early heat warnings, access to water and shade, cooling centres, and protecting people who work outdoors can save lives.

The question is whether that help arrives before the heat does.

Nine women. Nine families. And a country asking how this could happen again.

Over about nine weeks, the bodies of nine women have been found in and around Kempton Park, in Ekurhuleni, east of Johannesburg in South Africa. A specialised police team is now leading the investigation, and residents fear a serial killer may be responsible.

South Africa has some of the highest rates of violence against women in the world. Many South Africans call it a second pandemic.

On Heritage Day, President Cyril Ramaphosa spoke directly about it.

South Africans will be watching for more than words. They want arrests, protection, and justice.

## 7. Madagascar: one year after Gen Z toppled a president
- **Tier:** 3 · DEVELOPING
- **Tone:** reflective, neutral
- **Length:** about 75 s
- **Voice/music:** neutral voice at 1.0×; light, thoughtful bed
- **On-screen title:** MADAGASCAR'S GEN Z: "BETRAYED"?
- **Pronunciation:** Rajoelina = rah-dzoo-EH-lee-nah · Antananarivo = an-tah-nah-nah-REE-voo
- **Sources:** France 24 (24 Sept); Africanews (24 Sept); Amnesty International (Mar and Apr 2026); CBC

<<<
A year ago, young people in Madagascar brought down their president. Today, many of them say the revolution has betrayed them.

Madagascar is a large island nation off the south-east coast of Africa. In late September 2025, young protesters, calling themselves Gen Z, took to the streets over constant water and power cuts. The protests grew into a movement against the government.

Within weeks, President Andry Rajoelina had lost power, after an elite army unit sided with the protesters. The military took over, promising change.

One year on, activists say that change has not come. In April, four activists were arrested two days after joining a protest demanding a date for elections. Amnesty International says the military authorities are using vague charges, such as threatening national security, to silence critics.

The military authorities describe their rule as a transition.

Madagascar's story echoes across Africa, from Kenya to Morocco, where young people are using social media to demand jobs, services and a voice.

What happens next depends on one question. Will the military set a date to hand power back to civilians?

## 8. Morocco: a new first party, and most voters stayed home
- **Tier:** 3 · DEVELOPING
- **Tone:** neutral, explanatory
- **Length:** about 80 s
- **Voice/music:** neutral voice at 1.0×; light bed
- **On-screen title:** MOROCCO VOTED. MOST PEOPLE DIDN'T.
- **Pronunciation:** Akhannouch = ah-kha-NOOSH · Istiqlal = is-tik-LAHL
- **Sources:** Moroccan Interior Ministry provisional results via Maroc.ma; Hespress; Al Jazeera; The National; Morocco World News (all 24 Sept)

<<<
Morocco has a new first party, but six in ten voters stayed home.

Morocco is a kingdom in North Africa. Its King holds real power, but there is also an elected parliament of 395 seats. After each election, the King chooses the head of government from the party that wins the most seats.

On the twenty-third of September, Moroccans voted. Turnout was just 38 percent, down from 50 percent in 2021.

The Authenticity and Modernity Party, known as PAM, came first with 97 seats. The party that led the last government, the National Rally of Independents, fell from 102 seats to 66. And the Islamist Justice and Development Party made a comeback, from 13 seats to 54.

No party is close to the 198 seats needed for a majority. So the next step is coalition talks.

The low turnout matters. A year ago, young Moroccans in a movement called Gen Z 212 protested over jobs, hospitals and schools. Many now say voting doesn't change their lives.

If the King picks PAM's leader, Fatima-Zahra El Mansouri, she would be Morocco's first woman head of government.

## 9. Nigeria and the US: a deal on the minerals of the future
- **Tier:** 4 · OPPORTUNITY
- **Tone:** clear, forward-looking, balanced
- **Length:** about 75 s
- **Voice/music:** warm voice at 1.0×; light, upbeat bed
- **On-screen title:** NIGERIA'S $700 BILLION UNDERGROUND
- **Pronunciation:** Alake = ah-LAH-keh · Tinubu = tee-NOO-boo
- **Sources:** Premium Times; Mining Journal (UNGA sidelines, Sept 2026); UNGA coverage of Nigeria's Security Council call

<<<
Under Nigeria's soil lie minerals the whole world wants. This week, Nigeria signed a deal to dig into them.

Nigeria, in West Africa, is Africa's most populous country. It is best known for oil. But the government estimates its other mineral resources are worth around 700 billion dollars. They include critical minerals such as lithium, which is used to make batteries for phones and electric cars.

On the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York, Nigeria's Minister of Solid Minerals, Dele Alake, and US Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau signed a framework agreement. It aims to attract American investment into exploring, mapping and processing Nigeria's minerals.

Here is the key point. Nigeria says it does not want to simply export raw rock. It wants factories and processing at home, so more of the money and jobs stay in the country.

The big test will be whether that happens, and whether local communities near the mines actually benefit.

Nigeria also used the UN stage to demand at least two permanent seats for Africa on the UN Security Council, with veto power.

- **Sources:** SportPesa blog (25 Sept); Al Jazeera (9 Apr 2026); Malay Mail (10 Apr 2026); K24 Digital; Rio Times (match details)

<<<
Today in Nairobi, Kenya plays Eritrea in football. But for Eritrea, just getting onto the pitch is a story.

Kenya's national team, the Harambee Stars, hosts Eritrea at Nyayo Stadium in a qualifier for the 2027 Africa Cup of Nations. Kenya is co-hosting that tournament, with Uganda and Tanzania, so it has already qualified. These matches are its preparation.

For Eritrea, a small country on the Red Sea coast of the Horn of Africa, this match means much more. In March, Eritrea played its first international match in six years, and beat Eswatini to reach this stage.

But after that win, only three of the ten locally based players returned home. The other seven reportedly disappeared in South Africa on the way back.

It is not the first time. The United Nations estimates that around 80 Eritrean footballers, coaches and officials have defected during tournaments over two decades. They cite political repression, and military service with no end date.

So when Eritrea's players walk out today, many people will be watching the scoreline, and the team sheet on the way home.
