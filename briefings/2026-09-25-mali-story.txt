MALI: THE CAPITAL UNDER SIEGE
Africa Blind Spot | 25 September 2026

On Tuesday 22 September, Mali celebrated 66 years of independence.

Twelve days earlier, fighters linked to al-Qaeda overran an army camp at Dioura, in the centre of the country. More than 100 soldiers were killed. A security source told Reuters the toll was 130, with more than 100 more taken hostage. The group behind it later released video of captured soldiers.

In the capital, Bamako, people queue for fuel that armed groups have been trying to stop from reaching the city for a year.

This is Mali in 2026. Here is the story from the beginning.


FIRST, WHAT IS MALI?

Mali is a large, landlocked country in West Africa's Sahel, the dry belt just south of the Sahara. About 23 million people live there. Its capital, Bamako, is in the south, and the ancient city of Timbuktu is in the north.

Being landlocked matters in this story: Mali imports about 95% of its fuel by road, mostly from Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire. Whoever controls the roads can squeeze the whole country.


HOW THE CRISIS BEGAN (2012 TO 2021)

In 2012, Tuareg separatists in the north rose up, demanding an independent homeland they call Azawad. Jihadist groups joined them and soon took over the north. Soldiers angry at how the war was going overthrew the government.

France sent troops in 2013 and pushed the jihadists out of the northern cities. A UN peacekeeping mission followed. But the armed groups did not disappear. They spread into central Mali and into neighbouring Burkina Faso and Niger.

In 2020 and again in 2021, Colonel Assimi Goïta led military coups. He has ruled Mali ever since.


THE JUNTA'S BET

Goïta's government promised to win the war by doing things differently:

  It pushed out French troops (2022) and the UN peacekeepers (2023).

  It brought in Russian fighters, first the Wagner Group, now Russia's "Africa Corps".

  With Burkina Faso and Niger, also run by juntas, it formed the Alliance of Sahel States and left the West African bloc ECOWAS.

  It postponed elections, dissolved political parties and, in 2025, gave Goïta a new five-year term without a vote.

  It withdrew from the International Criminal Court, again together with Burkina Faso and Niger.

The promise was security. The results of the last 12 months tell a different story.


THE FUEL WAR

The main armed group now is JNIM (Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin), which is linked to al-Qaeda.

On 3 September 2025, JNIM announced a fuel blockade on cities in southern Mali. It began attacking tanker trucks on the roads from Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire. More than 300 tankers have been burned.

On 28 April 2026, JNIM declared a full blockade of Bamako.

The army fights back with armed convoys, escorted by Russian forces and Turkish-made drones. More than 1,000 tankers have reached the capital that way. But Bamako has fuel queues and rationing, with the military served first. With less fuel for power stations, the city is noticeably darker at night.


THE APRIL OFFENSIVE

On 25 April 2026, JNIM and the Tuareg separatist Azawad Liberation Front (FLA) launched coordinated attacks across Mali. It was the biggest offensive since 2012.

They struck Bamako, the military town of Kati next to it, and Mopti, Sévaré, Gao and Kidal.

Defence Minister General Sadio Camara was killed when a suicide bomber drove a car into his home in Kati. His wife and two grandchildren died with him.

The FLA took the northern city of Kidal, a city the army and its Russian allies had captured in 2023 and held up as a symbol of victory.

A group linked to the Islamic State used the chaos to launch attacks of its own.

More coordinated attacks followed in July.


THIS MONTH

10 September: JNIM overran the army camp at Dioura. Casualty figures vary by source:
  About 120 soldiers killed (a diplomatic source)
  130 killed and more than 100 taken hostage (a security source)
  150 killed (JNIM's own claim)
Five members of Russia's Africa Corps were also reportedly among the dead.

JNIM also says it struck air defences at Sévaré airport and killed 33 pro-government fighters around Sévaré and Konna.

14 September: A court sentenced journalist Abdramane Keïta, director of the newspaper Le Témoin, to two years in prison. He was arrested in June, days after saying on television that JNIM controlled a northern city. In August, another prominent journalist was jailed for seven years for criticising the junta. In Mali today, describing the war can land you in prison.

22 September: Mali marked 66 years of independence.


WHY IT MATTERS

For Malians: Fuel shortages mean higher prices, fewer hours of electricity, and harder lives for families already coping with one of the world's poorest economies.

For the Sahel: The Global Terrorism Index has named the Sahel the world's epicentre of terrorism. What happens in Mali spills over into its neighbours, and increasingly toward the coastal states of West Africa.

For the world: When Mali expelled the UN and French forces, much of the world's attention left with them. Journalists who report inside the country risk jail. Fewer eyes mean fewer people know what is happening.


WHAT TO WATCH

  Can the army keep fuel convoys reaching Bamako?

  Will the hostages from Dioura be released or exchanged?

  Does the JNIM–FLA partnership hold?

  How does Russia respond to losses among its Africa Corps?

  Do more journalists end up in prison?


THE BLIND SPOT

A capital city is being slowly starved of fuel by an al-Qaeda affiliate. More than 100 soldiers died in a single attack this month. A defence minister was assassinated in his home. And it is illegal, in practice, to say how bad it is.

That is why most of the world hasn't heard.
