## DR Congo: The plane that circled Kenge
*Kenge, south-western Democratic Republic of the Congo*

People on the ground in Kenge watched the plane go round, and then round again.

It was Friday, and the small aircraft had left the city of Kikwit bound for the capital, Kinshasa, when it developed a technical problem. The pilot turned towards Kenge, a town roughly halfway along the route and the capital of Kwango province. But Kenge has no landing strip. Witnesses said the plane circled several times, looking for somewhere to come down, before it lost control, struck a residential plot and burst into flames.

No one on board survived.

"All those on board died," said Major-General Efomi Ekenge, a spokesman for Congo's armed forces. "Twelve were burned beyond recognition, while the pilot's lifeless body was found outside the aircraft."

The exact number of dead is still unclear. The military spokesman put the toll at 14, a figure also reported by the Associated Press and Al Jazeera. Reuters, citing an official, reported 13 dead, including three officers. Initial reports did not say whether anyone on the ground was hurt.

Among the dead was General Likulia Bakumi, the auditor general of Congo's army, the senior officer responsible for military justice. The Associated Press reported that two senior military officials were killed, along with a major, a captain, members of an official delegation travelling with them, other soldiers and civilians. Their families will face a long and painful wait: most of the victims could not be recognised.

The aircraft was a Let L-410, a small twin-engine plane that is common on short routes across Africa. The aviation news site AeroTime reported that it was operated by Tracep Congo Aviation. Congo's aviation accident investigation authority says it is opening an inquiry into what went wrong.

For many Congolese, the crash is painfully familiar. The Democratic Republic of the Congo, a country roughly the size of Western Europe with few paved roads, depends heavily on small planes to connect its cities. Yet it has one of the worst air-safety records in the world. For years, the European Union has barred almost every airline certified in Congo from flying in its airspace, citing weak safety oversight. The International Air Transport Association, the global airline industry body, has called the country's record an embarrassment.

Congo's worst air disasters have fallen on people on the ground as well as those in the air. In 1996, a cargo plane crashed into a crowded market in Kinshasa, killing about 300 people. In 2008, a passenger jet ploughed into a neighbourhood of the eastern city of Goma.

The investigators' first task will be to establish what failed on board, and why a plane in trouble had nowhere safe to land.

In Kenge, residents are left with what they saw: a plane in trouble, circling their town in its final minutes, looking for a way down that never came.
