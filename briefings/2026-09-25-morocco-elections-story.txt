MOROCCO VOTES: THE STORY SO FAR
Africa Blind Spot | 25 September 2026

On a Wednesday in late September, polling stations opened across Morocco, from the busy streets of Casablanca to mountain villages in the Atlas and desert towns in the south. About 16 million people were registered to vote. By the end of the day, fewer than 4 in 10 of them had done so.

What happened that day, and what happens next, says a lot about where Morocco is heading. Here is the story from the beginning.


FIRST, HOW MOROCCO WORKS

Morocco is a kingdom. King Mohammed VI has reigned since 1999, and he still holds real power, especially over the army, religion and foreign policy.

Alongside the King there is an elected parliament. Its main chamber, the House of Representatives, has 395 seats. Every five years Moroccans vote to fill them: 305 seats are elected in local constituencies, and 90 are reserved for women, elected from regional lists.

The rule that matters most: after the election, the King chooses the head of government (the prime minister) from the party that won the most seats. That person must then build a coalition with enough partners to control at least 198 seats, a majority.

No Moroccan party has come close to winning a majority alone in recent decades, so every election ends in coalition bargaining.


THE LAST FIVE YEARS

At the last election, in 2021, a party called the National Rally of Independents, or RNI, came first. Its leader, billionaire businessman Aziz Akhannouch, became head of government and formed a coalition with two other parties: the Authenticity and Modernity Party (PAM) and the Istiqlal (Independence) Party, one of Morocco's oldest.

The big loser in 2021 was the Justice and Development Party (PJD), a moderate Islamist party that had led the government for ten years. It crashed from 125 seats to just 13.

Then came frustration. In late September 2025, young Moroccans organised online under the name "Gen Z 212" (212 is Morocco's phone code) and poured into the streets for about three weeks. Their complaints: too few jobs for young people, struggling hospitals and schools, inequality and corruption. They were especially angry that billions were going into football stadiums for the 2030 World Cup while public services suffered. Many protesters blamed Akhannouch directly and called for him to go.

In February 2026, Akhannouch stepped down as leader of his party. Mohamed Chaouki took over the RNI.

Just before the vote, there was another shock: a mass attempt by migrants to cross into Ceuta, a Spanish city on Morocco's northern coast, left dozens dead and strained relations with Spain and Europe.

That was the mood when Moroccans went to vote on 23 September 2026.


WHAT THE VOTERS DECIDED

The next day, the Interior Minister announced the provisional results:

  PAM (Authenticity and Modernity) ...... 97 seats  (up from 87)
  RNI (National Rally of Independents) .. 66 seats  (down from 102)
  Istiqlal .............................. 65 seats  (down from 81)
  PJD (Justice and Development) ......... 54 seats  (up from 13)
  Popular Movement ...................... 29 seats
  Socialist Union (USFP) ................ 26 seats
  Constitutional Union .................. 17 seats
  Smaller parties ....................... the rest

Four stories stand out.

1. PAM came first. PAM is seen as close to the palace. By winning the most seats, it earns the right to lead the next government.

2. The RNI was punished. The party that led the country lost more than a third of its seats. Many observers see this as voters holding the government responsible for the anger behind the Gen Z protests.

3. The Islamists came back. The PJD quadrupled its seats. Its veteran leader, Abdelilah Benkirane, called it "a great victory", but said the party will stay in opposition instead of joining the government.

4. Most people stayed home. Turnout was 38 percent, down from 50 percent in 2021 and one of the lowest since the King came to power. International observers from the Council of Europe said the voting itself ran smoothly, but the empty polling stations sent a message of their own: many Moroccans, especially the young, do not believe elections will change their lives.


WHAT HAPPENS NEXT

In the coming days, the King is expected to appoint a head of government from PAM. Two names are being discussed:

- Fatima-Zahra El Mansouri, PAM's leader. If chosen, she would be only the second woman ever to lead a government in the Arab world, after Tunisia's Najla Bouden.
- Fouzi Lekjaa, the budget minister, better known as head of Morocco's football federation.

Then comes the coalition puzzle. PAM has 97 seats and needs about 100 more.

The easiest option would be to repeat the old coalition with the RNI and Istiqlal (228 seats together). But PAM's leaders have ruled out working with the RNI again.

Without the RNI, PAM could still reach a majority. For example, PAM, Istiqlal, the Popular Movement and the Constitutional Union together hold 208 seats. A deal with the PJD looks very unlikely; the two parties have been rivals for years.

The results also remain provisional until Morocco's Constitutional Court rules on any challenges.


WHY IT MATTERS

This election was a test of whether Morocco's system can answer the frustrations that brought young people onto the streets a year ago. Voters changed who leads, but most did not vote at all.

The new government will face the same questions the Gen Z protesters asked: Where are the jobs? When will hospitals and schools improve? Who benefits from the big projects like the World Cup? How it answers will shape Morocco's next five years, and whether more Moroccans bother to vote in 2031.


KEY TERMS
- Head of government: Morocco's prime minister, chosen by the King from the largest party.
- Coalition: an alliance of parties that together hold a majority (198 of 395 seats).
- Turnout: the share of registered voters who actually voted.
- Gen Z 212: the youth protest movement of autumn 2025.
