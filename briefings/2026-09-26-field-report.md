## 1 · ETHIOPIA: The night Tigray went dark
**Mekelle, Tigray region, northern Ethiopia**

*What this story represents:* a peace deal collapsing, and a region cut off from the world again.
*Why it's a blind spot:* The last war here may have killed up to 600,000 people behind a communications blackout, and most of the world noticed too late. The lines went dead again on Thursday.

**THE SCENE**

On Thursday night, the phones in Mekelle stopped working.

"Last night telecommunications got knocked out," Duke Burbridge, an American aid worker in the city, told the BBC the next morning. The state operator, Ethio Telecom, went completely dark. Only the small private network, Safaricom, still carries a signal.

By Friday, the city of half a million people had the feel of a place bracing for a siege. In the market, Selam Fantay was selling garlic. "There is an atmosphere of fear," she told the Associated Press. "People are deeply concerned that the suspension of transport services signals the outbreak of renewed conflict."

The road east to the Afar region has been closed to civilians. According to reports, residents have been stocking up on groceries and queuing at banks. Hailay Meles, a businessman, is watching prices: "As long as this conflict persists, it will continue to drive up the prices of basic commodities."

Across the border in Afar, Valerie Browning, who has spent decades helping families there, says explosions were heard overnight and people are fleeing out of Tigray. "Too many people are being displaced."

**WHAT HAPPENED**
- **20 Sept:** Seven armed groups formed an alliance to overthrow Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. It includes Tigray's ruling party, the TPLF, and the Amhara militia Fano, which fought *against* Tigray in the last war.
- **22 Sept:** Government drone strikes hit two schools in the town of Alamata. Local authorities say at least 14 people were killed and 22 injured.
- **23 Sept:** Tigrayan forces seized the airports at Mekelle, Axum and Shire. Ethiopian Airlines cancelled every flight to the region. Fighting spread into Afar and Amhara, including the Kobo–Tumuga front.
- **24–25 Sept:** The TPLF declared a "full-blown war", and Tigray's networks went down. Reuters sources say the TPLF ordered the cut; a source close to the group denies it and blames the government.

**THE WEIGHT OF HISTORY**
Tigray has been here before. From 2020 to 2022, a war here was fought behind a communications blackout that hid famine from the world. The African Union's mediator, Olusegun Obasanjo, later said as many as 600,000 people may have died. More than 500,000 Tigrayans are still displaced from that war. In March, officials said over 1,300 of them had died of hunger and lack of medicine in the camps.

**BOTH SIDES**
The TPLF calls its war "defensive" and says government troops fired first. The federal government says the rebels launched an "all-out offensive". At the UN, President Taye Atske Selassie said Ethiopia had "exercised immense restraint" despite "interference".

**WHAT'S NEXT:** Whether neighbouring Eritrea is drawn in; whether the blackout lasts; whether food and aid convoys can still reach Tigray.

## 2 · SUDAN: A city watching the sky
**El Obeid, North Kordofan, central Sudan**

*What this story represents:* a city of half a million living under a sky full of drones.
*Why it's a blind spot:* The world's attention is on the diplomatic row over a visa. The people of El Obeid, where UN experts warn of a possible massacre, get far less of it.

**THE SCENE**

In El Obeid, people have learned to watch the sky.

Residents describe days when as many as 40 drones hang over the city at once. "You cannot hide from a drone strike," residents told CNN. A strike can find you asleep at home, on the way to school, or in the market.

This is a city of about half a million people. Nearly 100,000 of them already fled violence elsewhere and came here for safety. For about 18 months, according to UN officials, El Obeid has been almost completely surrounded. Fighters from the Rapid Support Forces, or RSF, an armed force separate from Sudan's army and now at war with it, hold most of the roads around the city.

Inside, UN officials report shortages of food, water, fuel and medicine, and a worsening cholera outbreak. Women and girls are reporting high rates of sexual violence.

UN experts have issued a stark warning: El Obeid could see a repeat of El Fasher. That Darfur city fell to the RSF last year, and the RSF killed some 6,000 civilians there.

**THIS WEEK'S NEWS**
- General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, the head of Sudan's army, was due to speak to world leaders at the UN General Assembly in New York. The United States never issued his visa.
- According to a Sudanese diplomatic source and a Western analyst quoted by Sudan Tribune, the visa was tied to one condition: signing a 90-day ceasefire proposed by US envoy Massad Boulos. Burhan reportedly refused.
- Sudan says barring a head of state violates the agreement that hosts the UN in New York. UN Secretary-General António Guterres expressed regret and again called for the war to end.

**THE BIG PICTURE**
Sudan's war began in April 2023, between the army and the RSF. It has forced millions of people from their homes. The US sanctioned Burhan in January 2025, citing abuses and obstruction of peace efforts.

**WHAT'S NEXT:** Whether the visa pressure brings a ceasefire closer or hardens positions, and whether the world acts before El Obeid becomes the next El Fasher.

- **Caption line:** Sudan's leader was kept out of the UN this week. In El Obeid, the war didn't pause. 🇸🇩

## 3 · MALI: The camp no one came to save
**Dioura, Mopti region, central Mali**

*What this story represents:* a government losing control of its own country, and silencing those who say so.
*Why it's a blind spot:* Journalists who describe the war are jailed, so one of Africa's deadliest conflicts goes largely unreported.

**THE SCENE**

Dioura is an army camp on the edge of Mali's Inner Niger Delta, the flood plain of rivers and cattle routes in the country's centre.

On the tenth of September, fighters from JNIM, an armed group linked to al-Qaeda, attacked it. According to security, military and local sources cited by AFP, the fighters came back the next day, and the army did not send reinforcements.

When it was over, at least 100 Malian soldiers were dead. A security source told Reuters the toll was 130, with more than 100 taken hostage. Five members of Russia's Africa Corps, a force controlled by the Russian government that fights beside Mali's army, were killed too. JNIM claims 150 dead and 93 captured, and it has released videos of dozens of young men it says are captured Malian soldiers.

The army has declined to comment, and the government has not released a death toll.

**THE SIEGE BEHIND IT**

The same group has spent a year choking Mali's capital, Bamako, of fuel. Mali has no coastline. Almost all of its fuel arrives by truck, and JNIM has burned more than 300 tankers on the roads.

Last October, the capital's residents described what that feels like:
- "You cannot get to your job if you don't have gas to put in your car," said Oumar Konipo, 73, describing five- or six-hour queues for 20 litres of fuel.
- A bank employee said he had been to more than 20 petrol stations and found none.
- The government closed every school and university in the country for two weeks.

Today, fuel reaches Bamako in armed convoys. The city is darker at night.

**THE SILENCE**
On 14 September, a court sentenced journalist Abdramane Keïta to two years in prison. He had said on television that JNIM controlled a northern city. In Mali, describing the war can cost you your freedom.

**BOTH SIDES**
Mali's military leader, General Assimi Goïta, used Independence Day on 22 September to promise "proactive" operations, a better-equipped army and "dialogue and reconciliation". His government argues a decade of French and UN operations failed. But the Africa Center for Strategic Studies says militant-linked deaths in Mali have tripled since the military took power.

**WHAT'S NEXT:** The fate of the captured soldiers, whether the convoys keep getting through, and how long the army's loyalty holds.

**READY TO POST**
- **Hook:** *They attacked. They came back the next day. No one came to help.*
- **Caption line:** More than 100 soldiers killed in one attack, and in Mali, saying so on TV can land you in prison. 🇲🇱

## 4 · DR CONGO: Inside the Ebola fight
**Bunia, Ituri province, north-eastern DR Congo**

*What this story represents:* a disease being pushed back, and the people risking their lives to do it.
*Why it's a blind spot:* Ebola headlines focus on case counts. The burial teams, nurses and grieving families who decide whether the outbreak ends rarely make the news.

**THE SCENE**

In Bunia, the burial teams go out nearly every day.

They dig graves, prepare bodies and carry coffins for the people Ebola has killed. They are exhausted. They told the Associated Press they are at their limits, with a rising workload and communities that sometimes turn on them.

For families, the hardest part is often what they cannot do. Ebola spreads through the body fluids of the sick and the dead, so traditional funeral rites like washing and touching the body are too dangerous. Researchers found that relatives feel shut out of burying their own.

That grief has boiled over. In May, a crowd at a hospital in Mongbwalu demanded that bodies be returned for traditional burial, and set fire to a tent used by Doctors Without Borders.

On the front line, fear is constant. "We don't have protective equipment," a community nurse in Bunia said. "We're extremely afraid."

**THE NUMBERS**
- **7,773** confirmed cases and **3,759** deaths as of 22 September. It is the second-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded.
- The strain is called **Bundibugyo**. There is no approved vaccine or treatment for it.
- Last weekend, vaccinations began for health workers in Bunia, using a vaccine made for a different strain, as part of a trial.

**THE FIRST SIGNS OF HOPE**
The World Health Organization says that over the last three weeks:
- cases fell about **26 percent** in Ituri,
- cases fell about **15 percent** in Haut-Uélé,
- but cases rose **73 percent** in North Kivu.

Congo is also running short of health workers to staff treatment centres, especially in North Kivu.

**WHAT'S NEXT:** Whether Ituri's decline holds, and whether North Kivu, already scarred by conflict, becomes the new centre of the outbreak.

**READY TO POST**
- **Hook:** *In Bunia, the burial teams go out nearly every day. For the first time in months, there are fewer graves to dig.*
- **Caption line:** Ebola is falling where it started, and surging somewhere new. 🇨🇩

## 5 · SOUTH AFRICA: The women of Kempton Park
**Kempton Park, Ekurhuleni, east of Johannesburg**

*What this story represents:* a community of women demanding the right to feel safe.
*Why it's a blind spot:* Violence against women in South Africa is so common it often goes unreported abroad. Nine killings in nine weeks shows how deep the crisis runs.

**THE SCENE**

When word spread that another woman had been found, the women of Dawn Park rushed out of their homes, some still in slippers, dressing gowns and blankets. That's how reporters described the moment the ninth body was discovered.

Since July, nine women have been murdered in and around Kempton Park. Their bodies were left near rivers and roads. The first five were found in the Kempton Park area and the others 25 to 35 kilometres away.

Women here have changed how they live. They walk in groups, or ask men they trust to walk them through their own neighbourhoods. "We are living in fear," women told a local radio station. "Everything is scary," another said to the Sunday Times.

On 17 September, police barricaded parts of the area and launched a manhunt. Acting Police Minister Firoz Cachalia has promised a major crackdown across Ekurhuleni: more roadblocks, more visible policing, more resources.

**THE PRESIDENT'S WORDS**
On Heritage Day, President Cyril Ramaphosa said, "I am ashamed of what we men are doing to the women of South Africa." He added: "The heritage of our women is that they must be able to walk around without any fear that they will be attacked, that they will be raped, that they will be killed."

He has promised to address the nation within days.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
South Africa has some of the highest rates of violence against women in the world. For many South Africans, this case feels like the whole crisis in one place.

**WHAT'S NEXT:** Ramaphosa's national address, and whether police make an arrest.
