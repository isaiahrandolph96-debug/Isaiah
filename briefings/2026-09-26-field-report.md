## Ethiopia: The night Tigray went dark
*Mekelle, northern Ethiopia*

The phones in Mekelle stopped working on Thursday night.

"Last night telecommunications got knocked out," Duke Burbridge, an American aid worker in the city, told the BBC the next morning. Ethio Telecom, the state-owned network that most people rely on, went completely dark across Tigray, Ethiopia's northernmost region. Only a much smaller private network, Safaricom, still carried a signal.

The blackout came at the end of a week in which a four-year peace deal came apart.

On Sunday, seven armed groups from across the country announced an alliance to overthrow Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. Among them are Tigray's ruling party, the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF), and an Amhara militia called Fano that fought against Tigray in the last war. On Tuesday, according to local authorities, government drone strikes hit two schools in the town of Alamata, killing at least 14 people and injuring 22. On Wednesday, Tigrayan forces seized the airports at Mekelle, Axum and Shire, and Ethiopian Airlines cancelled every flight to the region. By Friday, the TPLF had declared what it called a "full-blown war".

Who cut the networks is disputed. Four people with knowledge of the matter told Reuters the TPLF ordered the shutdown. A source close to the group denied that and blamed the government.

In Mekelle's market, Selam Fantay was still selling garlic. "There is an atmosphere of fear," she told the Associated Press. "People are deeply concerned that the suspension of transport services signals the outbreak of renewed conflict." The road east to the neighbouring Afar region has been closed to civilians. Hailay Meles, a local businessman, said the disruption would only push prices higher.

Just across that border, Valerie Browning of the Afar Pastoralist Development Association said explosions had been heard overnight and people were fleeing out of Tigray. "Too many people are being displaced," she said.

For people in Tigray, a dead phone line carries a particular dread. When war last came, between 2020 and 2022, the region was sealed off for two years, and famine spread largely out of sight. The African Union's mediator, former Nigerian president Olusegun Obasanjo, later said as many as 600,000 people may have died. More than half a million Tigrayans are still living in displacement camps, and in March regional officials said more than 1,300 of them had died of hunger and lack of medicine.

Each side blames the other for the new fighting. The TPLF says government troops fired first and calls its campaign "defensive". The federal government says the rebels launched an "all-out offensive". Speaking at the United Nations this week, President Taye Atske Selassie said Ethiopia had "exercised immense restraint" in the face of outside "interference".

The African Union has called for an immediate end to the fighting. Diplomats are watching neighbouring Eritrea, once Ethiopia's ally and now its rival, for any sign of being drawn in.

## Sudan: A city that watches the sky
*El Obeid, central Sudan*

In El Obeid, people have learned to look up.

On some days, residents say, as many as 40 drones hang over the city at once. "You cannot hide from a drone strike," residents told CNN. A strike can find you asleep at home, on the way to school, or in the market.

About half a million people live in El Obeid, the main city of Sudan's North Kordofan state. Nearly 100,000 of them had already fled fighting elsewhere and came here believing it was safe. For about 18 months, according to UN officials, the city has been almost completely surrounded by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF). The RSF is an armed force that grew out of militias in Darfur, and it has been at war with Sudan's army since April 2023.

UN officials report shortages of food, water, fuel and medicine inside the city, and a worsening cholera outbreak. Women and girls are reporting high rates of sexual violence. UN experts have warned that El Obeid could suffer a repeat of what happened in El Fasher, a city in Darfur that fell to the RSF last year, where the RSF killed some 6,000 civilians.

This week, Sudan's war reached New York in a different way. General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, the head of Sudan's army and its de facto leader, was due to address world leaders at the United Nations General Assembly. The United States never issued his visa.

According to a Sudanese diplomatic source and a Western analyst quoted by the Sudan Tribune, the visa was tied to one condition: that Burhan sign a 90-day ceasefire proposed by the American envoy Massad Boulos. Burhan reportedly refused. Sudan's ambassador to the UN said blocking a head of state breaks the agreement under which the United States hosts the UN. Secretary-General António Guterres expressed regret over the decision, and again called for the war to end.

Washington placed sanctions on Burhan in January 2025, accusing his forces of abuses and of blocking peace efforts.

Whether this pressure brings a ceasefire closer, or hardens both sides, may decide what happens next in El Obeid.

## Mali: The camp no one came to save
*Dioura, central Mali*

The fighters came to the army camp at Dioura on the tenth of September. The next day, they came back. No reinforcements arrived.

That account comes from security, military and local sources cited by the AFP news agency. When the fighting ended, at least 100 Malian soldiers were dead. A security source told Reuters the toll was 130, with more than 100 taken hostage. Five members of Russia's Africa Corps, a force controlled by the Russian government that fights alongside Mali's army, were also killed.

The attackers belonged to JNIM, an alliance of armed groups linked to al-Qaeda that has fought across West Africa's Sahel region for years. The group claims it killed 150 soldiers and captured 93, and it has released videos of dozens of young men it says are Malian prisoners. Mali's army declined to comment, and the government has not published a death toll.

Dioura sits on the edge of the Inner Niger Delta, a wide plain of rivers and cattle routes in Mali's Mopti region, halfway between the capital, Bamako, and the desert north. Whoever controls this middle ground controls movement across the country.

JNIM has also spent a year trying to starve the capital of fuel. Mali has no coastline, so almost all of its fuel arrives by truck from Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire, and the group has burned more than 300 tankers on those roads. Last October, the effect reached every home in Bamako. "You cannot get to your job if you don't have gas to put in your car," Oumar Konipo, a 73-year-old retired resident, said at the time. He described waiting five or six hours for 20 litres of fuel. The government closed every school and university in the country for two weeks. Today, fuel reaches Bamako in armed convoys, and the city is noticeably darker at night.

Mali has been run by the military since coups in 2020 and 2021. Its leader, General Assimi Goïta, expelled French troops and United Nations peacekeepers and turned to Russia, promising that Malians would finally be safe. On Independence Day this week, he pledged more "proactive" military operations, better-equipped forces and "dialogue and reconciliation".

Independent researchers see a different picture. The Africa Center for Strategic Studies, a research body attached to the US Defense Department, says deaths linked to armed groups in Mali have tripled since the military took power.

Telling that story inside Mali carries a risk of its own. On 14 September, a court sentenced the journalist Abdramane Keïta to two years in prison. He had said on television that JNIM controlled a city in the north.

> - **Blind spot:** Jailed journalists mean one of Africa's deadliest wars goes largely unreported.

## DR Congo: The people fighting Ebola one burial at a time
*Bunia, north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo*

Nearly every day, the burial teams of Bunia go out to dig graves.

They prepare the bodies of people killed by Ebola, carry the coffins and bury the dead safely, so the virus cannot spread through the funeral. They told the Associated Press they are exhausted, and at their limits. The work keeps growing, and the communities they serve do not always welcome them.

Ebola spreads through contact with the body fluids of the sick and the dead. That means the rites many families hold sacred, washing and touching the body of someone they love, are too dangerous. Researchers who interviewed families found that many feel shut out of burying their own relatives. In May, that grief turned to anger in the town of Mongbwalu, where a crowd demanding the return of bodies set fire to a tent used by Doctors Without Borders.

Those on the front line are frightened too. "We don't have protective equipment," a community nurse in Bunia said. "We're extremely afraid."

Bunia is the main city of Ituri province, the centre of the second-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded. As of 22 September, the Democratic Republic of the Congo had confirmed 7,773 cases and 3,759 deaths since the outbreak began in May. It is caused by a strain called Bundibugyo, for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment. Last weekend, health workers in Bunia began receiving a vaccine designed for a different strain, as part of a trial.

This week brought the first real sign of progress. The World Health Organization said new cases had fallen by about 26 percent in Ituri and about 15 percent in neighbouring Haut-Uélé over the past three weeks.

But in North Kivu province, further south, cases rose by 73 percent over the same period. North Kivu has been torn by armed conflict for years, which makes tracing contacts and treating patients far harder. The WHO says Congo is also running short of health workers to staff its treatment centres, especially there.

The coming weeks will show whether the progress in Ituri holds, and whether North Kivu becomes the new centre of the outbreak.

## South Africa: The women of Kempton Park
*Kempton Park, east of Johannesburg*

When word spread that another woman had been found, the women of Dawn Park ran out of their homes, some still in slippers, dressing gowns and blankets.

She was the ninth. Since July, nine women have been murdered in and around Kempton Park, a suburb in the Ekurhuleni area east of Johannesburg. Their bodies were left near rivers and roads. The first five were found in the Kempton Park area and the others 25 to 35 kilometres away. Police are investigating whether one person is responsible.

The killings have changed daily life for women here. Many now walk only in groups, or ask men they trust to walk them through their own neighbourhoods. "We are living in fear," women told a local radio station. "Everything is scary," another woman told the Sunday Times.

On 17 September, police sealed off parts of the area and launched a manhunt. The acting police minister, Firoz Cachalia, has promised a major crackdown across Ekurhuleni, with more roadblocks, more officers on the streets and more resources for detectives.

South Africa has some of the highest rates of violence against women in the world. For many South Africans, Kempton Park has become the crisis in miniature.

President Cyril Ramaphosa addressed it directly on Heritage Day, a national holiday on 24 September. "I am ashamed of what we men are doing to the women of South Africa," he said. "The heritage of our women is that they must be able to walk around without any fear that they will be attacked, that they will be raped, that they will be killed." He has promised to speak to the nation within days about what the government will do.

For the women of Kempton Park, the measure will be simpler: an arrest, and the chance to walk home alone again.
