ETHIOPIA: THE WAR THAT CAME BACK
Africa Blind Spot | 25 September 2026

On the morning of Wednesday 23 September, fighters in Tigray, in Ethiopia's far north, took control of three airports: Mekelle, Axum and Shire. Within hours, Ethiopian Airlines cancelled every flight to the region. The internet began to cut out across the north.

For millions of people in Tigray, it felt horribly familiar. Less than four years ago, a war here killed hundreds of thousands of people. Now the peace that ended it is falling apart.

Here is the story from the beginning.


FIRST, WHO IS WHO

Ethiopia is Africa's second most populous country, a nation of more than 120 million people and many ethnic groups. It is divided into regions, each largely run by one of those groups.

Three names matter in this story:

  ABIY AHMED is Ethiopia's prime minister. He came to power in 2018 and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2019 for ending a long standoff with neighbouring Eritrea. His Prosperity Party runs the federal government in the capital, Addis Ababa.

  The TPLF (Tigray People's Liberation Front) is the main party in Tigray. It dominated Ethiopia's government for almost 30 years before Abiy took over. Its fighters are called the Tigray Defense Forces (TDF).

  FANO is an armed movement from the Amhara region, next door to Tigray. It fought on the government's side in the last war, then turned against it.


THE LAST WAR (2020 TO 2022)

In November 2020, after months of rising tension, Abiy sent the army into Tigray. Troops from Eritrea and fighters from the Amhara region joined the federal side.

It became one of the deadliest wars of this century. Tigray was cut off from food, medicine, banking and the internet. Famine spread. The African Union's mediator, former Nigerian president Olusegun Obasanjo, later said as many as 600,000 people may have died. Nobody knows the exact number.

On 2 November 2022, the government and the TPLF signed a peace deal in Pretoria, South Africa. The guns went mostly quiet.

But the deal left the hardest questions unanswered: who controls disputed land (especially Western Tigray, which Amhara forces took during the war), what happens to the fighters, and who is held responsible for the killings. Human Rights Watch says key promises were broken for years, including the forced removal of ethnic Tigrayans from Western Tigray.


FOUR UNEASY YEARS

After the deal, old allies became enemies:

  Fano, angry at the peace deal and at plans to disband regional forces, took up arms against the government in Amhara.

  Ethiopia and Eritrea fell out. Ethiopia now accuses Eritrea of backing armed groups against it. Eritrea denies this.

  In recent months, clashes and drone strikes near Tigray raised fears of a new war.

In June 2026, Ethiopia held a national election. Abiy's Prosperity Party won 438 seats, a landslide. But nobody in Tigray could vote, because of the unsettled situation there.


THIS WEEK

Saturday 20 September: seven armed groups from across Ethiopia, including rebels from Tigray, Amhara, Afar and Oromia, announced a new alliance. They call it the Ethiopian People's Forces Alliance for Survival. Its goal: to overthrow Abiy's government and set up a transitional government.

Tuesday 22 September: fighting broke out between federal troops and TPLF-aligned forces. Local authorities in Tigray said a strike on the town of Alamata killed at least 14 people and injured 22. The town's mayor accused federal forces of targeting schools.

Wednesday 23 September: Tigrayan forces seized the three airports and attacked government positions in the neighbouring Afar and Amhara regions. A TPLF spokesman said the Pretoria peace deal was no longer valid.

Each side blames the other for starting it. The TPLF says government forces fired first and calls its fight "defensive". The government says the rebels ordered an "all-out offensive".

The same day, in New York, Ethiopia's President Taye Atske Selassie told the United Nations that Ethiopia had "exercised immense restraint" despite "continued interference in our internal affairs by adversarial elements". His words appeared aimed at Eritrea and Egypt.


WHY IT MATTERS

Ethiopia is the anchor of the Horn of Africa. A war that spreads across several regions at once could:

  Put millions of civilians at risk again, in places that have barely recovered from the last war and its famine.

  Pull in Eritrea, and possibly other neighbours, in a region already shaken by the war in Sudan.

  Cut people off from the outside world again. During the last war, Tigray's internet was switched off for about two years, and much of the suffering went unseen.


WHAT TO WATCH

  Does fighting spread further into Amhara, Afar or Oromia?

  Do Eritrean troops cross the border?

  Do the African Union and other mediators manage to restart talks?

  Do flights, phone lines and internet in the north come back on?


THE BLIND SPOT

The last Tigray war was one of the deadliest conflicts on earth, and much of the world barely noticed until famine was already under way. The warning signs this time are loud. The question is whether anyone is listening.
