# The Night the Lines Went Dead
### Ethiopia's war is back. Tigray has been here before.
**Africa Blind Spot · 25 September 2026**

---

On Thursday night in Mekelle, the phones stopped working.

No bars. No messages. No way to call a mother in Addis Ababa, a brother in the diaspora or a cousin across town to say *we're okay.*

"Last night telecommunications got knocked out," Duke Burbridge, an American aid worker in the city, told the BBC the next morning.

For most of the world, it was one line in a news report. For the millions of people in Tigray, it was a sound they already knew. It was the sound of the last time.

---

## The last time

The last time started in November 2020.

The phones went dead then too. So did the banks, so you couldn't pay for bread. So did the internet, so no one outside could see what was happening inside.

For two years, Tigray was a region behind a wall. Food ran out. Medicine ran out. Famine came, quietly, where no camera could film it.

When the African Union's mediator, Olusegun Obasanjo, later tried to count the dead, the number he gave was *around 600,000.*

Six hundred thousand. That's more than the population of many capital cities: mothers, farmers, students, grandfathers, children. Most of the world never learned a single one of their names.

Then, on 2 November 2022, in Pretoria, the two sides signed a peace deal. The guns went mostly quiet.

People began, carefully, to hope.

---

## The peace that never quite came

Peace on paper is not the same as peace at home.

More than half a million Tigrayans never made it home. They are still living in displacement camps four years later. They were promised they could return to Western Tigray, the fertile land where many of them had been driven out. Human Rights Watch says the expulsions kept happening instead.

Aid shrank. In March this year, regional officials said **more than 1,300 people in those camps had died of hunger and lack of medicine.** They survived a war, only to die waiting for it to really end.

In June, the displaced marched to the government offices in Mekelle and protested for three days. Their demand was simple: *let us go home.*

That same month, Ethiopia held a national election. Tigray didn't take part.

---

## Old enemies, new war

Then, this week, everything moved at once.

**Sunday, 20 September:** Seven armed groups from across Ethiopia announced an alliance to overthrow Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. Among them were Tigray's fighters and Amhara's Fano militia, which fought *against* Tigray in the last war. The men who once aimed their rifles at each other now share a cause.

**Tuesday, 22 September:** Government drones struck the town of Alamata. Two of the strikes hit schools. At least 14 people were killed and 22 injured, according to local authorities.

Schools. Places parents send their children because they believe they are safe.

**Wednesday, 23 September:** Tigrayan forces seized the airports at Mekelle, Axum and Shire. Every flight to Tigray was cancelled.

On the same day, on the other side of the world, Ethiopia's president stood before world leaders at the United Nations and said his country had "exercised immense restraint."

**Thursday night:** The lines went dead.

---

## Friday in Mekelle

Selam Fantay sells garlic in the market in Mekelle. On Friday she told a reporter what the city felt like:

> *"There is an atmosphere of fear."*

The road to the neighbouring Afar region has been closed to civilians. Hailay Meles, a businessman, is watching prices climb:

> *"As long as this conflict persists, it will continue to drive up the prices of basic commodities."*

Garlic. Bread. Fuel. This is how a war arrives in ordinary life: not only with explosions, but with a market stall where everything suddenly costs more, and a phone that shows no signal.

And people are leaving. Valerie Browning, who has spent decades helping families in Afar, says explosions were heard overnight and people are fleeing across the border into her region:

> *"Too many people are being displaced."*

---

## Imagine

Imagine you are one of those 500,000 people still in a camp.

You survived the siege. You buried people you loved. You waited four years to go home. You protested. You hoped.

And on Thursday night, your phone went dark again.

You don't know yet if the fighting will reach you. You don't know if the aid trucks will keep coming. An earlier strike already destroyed a convoy carrying grain to north-western Tigray. You don't know if anyone outside can hear you.

Last time, for two years, they mostly couldn't.

---

## Why it's bigger than Tigray

This is not only Tigray's story.

Ethiopia is a country of more than 120 million people. The new alliance has fighters in the north, west, south and east.

Next door, Eritrea fought *alongside* Ethiopia last time. Now the two are rivals. Abiy says it is only "a matter of time" before Ethiopia regains access to Eritrea's port of Assab. Eritrea hears that as a threat.

And across the other border, Sudan is already in its fourth year of war.

The African Union has called for the fighting to stop. The UN and the EU have urged restraint. The US has warned of sanctions. Each side still blames the other for firing first.

---

## The blind spot

Here is the part that should stay with you.

The last war in Tigray may have killed up to 600,000 people, and much of the world only started paying attention when the famine was already killing people.

The warning signs are not hidden this time. There are airports seized, schools hit, roads closed, lines cut and families fleeing. It is all happening in plain sight, this week.

The only question left is whether we look.
