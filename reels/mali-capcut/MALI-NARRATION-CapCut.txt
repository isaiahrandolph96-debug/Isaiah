=== PART 1: THE ATTACK ===

On the tenth of September, the fighters came for Dioura.

Dioura is a small army camp in central Mali, in the Mopti region. It sits on the edge of the Inner Niger Delta, where farmland, rivers and cattle routes connect the south of the country to the desert north.

The attackers belonged to JNIM, a coalition of armed groups linked to al-Qaeda. By the time it was over, Mali's army had suffered one of its worst defeats in years.

How many died? The answer depends on who you ask.

A security source told Reuters that 130 soldiers were killed, and more than 100 were taken hostage. A diplomatic source put the toll at around 120. JNIM itself claimed 150 dead, including two colonels, and said it had captured 93 soldiers.

The group then released videos of dozens of men it said were captured Malian soldiers.

Mali's army declined to comment. The government has not published a death toll.

Among the dead, according to several reports, were five members of Russia's Africa Corps, the Kremlin-controlled force that replaced the Wagner Group in Mali.

This was not the first time Dioura had been hit. The same camp was attacked in 2023, and again in 2025. Each time, the army came back. Each time, the fighters returned.

And Dioura was not alone. In August, JNIM attacked the garrison at San, further south, killing ten soldiers. This month, the group also claimed to have struck air defence systems at the airport in Sévaré, the army's most important hub in central Mali.

Twelve days after Dioura, on the twenty-second of September, Mali celebrated sixty-six years of independence.


=== PART 2: HOW MALI GOT HERE ===

To understand how Mali got here, you have to go back to 2012.

That year, Tuareg separatists in the north rose up, demanding their own homeland, which they call Azawad. Jihadist groups joined the uprising, then took it over. Within months, the ancient cities of the north, including Timbuktu, were under jihadist control. In the capital, angry soldiers overthrew the government.

In 2013, France sent troops. They pushed the jihadists out of the northern cities. A United Nations peacekeeping mission followed. In 2015, the government and northern armed groups signed a peace deal in Algiers.

But the fighters did not disappear. They moved south, into central Mali, and across the borders into Burkina Faso and Niger.

In central Mali, the war took on a darker shape. A local jihadist unit called Katiba Macina recruited among Fulani herders, a community that had long felt ignored by the state. Other communities formed their own militias. Villages were attacked because of who lived in them. The killing became personal.

In 2020, and again in 2021, a young colonel named Assimi Goïta led military coups. He has ruled Mali ever since.

Goïta made the people of Mali a promise. The French and the UN had failed, he said. Mali would take back its sovereignty and win the war on its own terms.

So his government expelled French troops. It ended the United Nations peacekeeping mission. It brought in Russian fighters, first from the Wagner Group, and now from Russia's Africa Corps. It ended the Algiers peace deal with the northern groups in January 2024. It left the West African bloc ECOWAS, and formed a new alliance with the military governments of Burkina Faso and Niger.

Elections were postponed. Political parties were dissolved. In 2025, Goïta was given a new five-year term without a vote.

The promise was security. Five years later, the Africa Center for Strategic Studies, a research institution of the United States Defense Department, says annual deaths linked to militant Islamist groups in Mali have tripled since the military took power.


=== PART 3: THE SIEGE OF THE CAPITAL ===

Mali has no coastline. Almost all of its fuel, around ninety-five percent, arrives by truck, on long roads from Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire.

JNIM understood what that meant.

In September 2025, the group announced a fuel blockade. Its fighters began ambushing tanker trucks on the highways into southern Mali. More than 300 tankers have been burned.

Within weeks, the effects reached the capital, Bamako. Petrol stations ran dry. Queues stretched for hours.

"You cannot get to your job if you don't have gas to put in your car," one retired resident of Bamako said at the time. He described people spending five or six hours in line, just to get twenty litres of fuel.

A bank employee told reporters he had been to more than twenty petrol stations, and still could not find any fuel.

In late October 2025, the government closed every school and university in the country for two weeks, because teachers and students could not travel.

In April 2026, JNIM declared a full blockade of Bamako.

The army fought back the only way it could: with armed convoys. Soldiers, Russian forces and drones now escort tanker trucks into the capital. More than a thousand have made it through. But fuel is rationed, and the military comes first. With less fuel for power stations, Bamako is darker at night than it used to be.

The Africa Center describes the campaign as an explicit economic strategy to isolate the capital. JNIM does not need to capture Bamako. It only needs to make the state too expensive to run.


=== PART 4: THE WAR REACHES THE STATE ===

On the twenty-fifth of April 2026, Mali woke up to gunfire.

In a coordinated offensive, JNIM and the Tuareg separatist Azawad Liberation Front struck targets across the country, including Bamako, the garrison town of Kati, Mopti, Gao and Kidal. It was the largest offensive in Mali since 2012.

In Kati, a suicide bomber drove a car into the home of the defence minister, General Sadio Camara. He was killed, along with his wife and two grandchildren.

In the north, the separatists took back Kidal, the city the army and its Russian allies had captured in 2023 and celebrated as a symbol of victory.

A rival group linked to the Islamic State used the chaos to launch attacks of its own.

Think about what that alliance means. JNIM wants a Mali ruled by its version of Islamic law. The separatists want independence for the north. They want different futures. But for now, they are fighting the same government.

"The alliance between JNIM and FLA poses a threat to the government's consolidation of national territory," Beverly Ochieng, a senior analyst at Control Risks, told Al Jazeera.


=== PART 5: CAUGHT IN THE MIDDLE ===

In a war like this, civilians are caught between every side.

On the fifteenth of June 2026, an airstrike by Russia's Africa Corps hit the village of Kyrnia, in the Mopti region. According to Human Rights Watch, eight civilians were killed, including three children.

On the tenth of July, more than a hundred Africa Corps fighters, accompanied by Malian soldiers, entered the village of Bombori, also in Mopti. Human Rights Watch says they rounded up at least eighty men and boys from the Fulani neighbourhood under a tree. Nine civilians were killed, including four children. One of them was beheaded.

In mid-September, local sources cited in media reports said an army unit executed eleven civilians in Ke-Macina, in the Ségou region, and threw their bodies into a river in sacks. That report has not been independently confirmed.

These are not the first allegations. In March 2022, in the village of Moura, a United Nations human rights investigation found that more than five hundred people were killed, most of them summarily executed, during a five-day operation by Malian troops and foreign military personnel. The government refused to let UN investigators enter the village.

Jihadist groups and ethnic militias have also massacred civilians in central Mali, for years.

Analysts have long warned about a vicious cycle. When a village is punished for supposedly helping the militants, it gains a new reason to help them. Every abuse becomes a recruiting tool.

Today, around 415,000 people are displaced inside Mali. A food security assessment in June found that forty percent of households needed humanitarian help. Only four percent had received any in the previous three months. The United Nations appeal for Mali was about seven percent funded.


=== PART 6: THE SILENCE ===

There is another reason most of the world has not heard this story.

In Mali today, describing the war can cost you your freedom.

On the fourteenth of September, a court sentenced the journalist Abdramane Keïta, director of the newspaper Le Témoin, to two years in prison. He had been arrested in June, days after he said on television that JNIM controlled a city in the north. Human Rights Watch called the charges bogus.

In August, another prominent journalist was sentenced to seven years for criticising the government.

In late 2025, the authorities suspended French television channels, accusing them of false reports about the fuel blockade.

The Africa Center warns that because of these restrictions, the true number of deaths across the Sahel is probably higher than the official counts.

When it is dangerous to tell the truth, the truth travels slowly.


=== PART 7: WHAT COMES NEXT ===

On Independence Day, General Goïta promised to intensify the fight. He spoke of proactive military operations, army reforms, modern equipment and better training. He reaffirmed Mali's alliance with Burkina Faso and Niger. He called for dialogue and reconciliation among Malians, and he said Mali would keep defending its sovereign choices.

His government argues that ten years of French and United Nations operations did not bring peace, and that Malians must decide their own future. That message has drawn real support in Bamako.

But the pressure is growing. The Sahel now accounts for forty-two percent of all deaths linked to militant Islamist groups in Africa. JNIM is pushing south, toward the coastal countries of West Africa. In June, it attacked the airport in Niamey, the capital of neighbouring Niger.

So watch for these signs. Can the convoys keep Bamako supplied? What happens to the soldiers captured at Dioura? Does the alliance between the jihadists and the separatists hold? And how long can a military government keep its army's loyalty while it keeps losing soldiers?

A capital under siege. A defence minister killed in his own home. More than a hundred soldiers lost in a single attack. And journalists in prison for describing it.

Mali is not quiet. It has been silenced.
