# The King Who Sailed West

A king gave up his throne for a question.

Seven hundred years ago, in West Africa, there was an empire called Mali.
It stretched from the Atlantic coast to the edge of the Sahara Desert, across land that is now Mali, Senegal, Guinea and more.
And it sat on some of the richest gold fields on Earth.

Its emperor, called the Mansa, wanted for nothing.
Except an answer.
What lies on the other side of the ocean?

So he built a fleet.
Two hundred ships filled with men.
Two hundred more, loaded with gold, water and food.
He sent them west, into the Atlantic.

They were gone a long time.
Then one ship came back.
Its captain said that out in the open sea there was something like a river, a powerful current, and it had carried the others away.
They were never seen again.

Any other king would have stopped.
This one built more.

Two thousand ships.
One thousand for his men. One thousand for water and supplies.
And this time, the Mansa went himself.
He left a deputy to rule until he returned.
He sailed west.
And he never came back.

We only know this because of that deputy.
His name was Mansa Musa.

In 1324, Musa crossed Africa on Hajj, the holy journey to Mecca.
In Cairo, Egypt, he gave away so much gold that its value fell for years.
There, he told an Egyptian official how he became king.
A historian named al-Umari wrote his words down.
It is the only record we have.

No wreck has ever been found.
No proof has been found in the Americas.
Some historians believe the story. Others think Musa told it to explain how he took the throne.
Even the king's name is a mystery. History calls him Abu Bakr the Second, but Musa never said his name.

Did he reach the coast of Brazil?
Did the ocean take him?
We may never know.

But this much was written down:
Nearly two hundred years before Columbus, an African king looked out at the Atlantic and wanted to know what was on the other side.

He gave up a throne built on gold.
Not for more gold.
For a question.

# The King Who Sailed West (60-second cut for Reels/TikTok)

A king gave up his throne for a question.

Seven hundred years ago, the Mali Empire of West Africa sat on some of the richest gold fields on Earth.
But its emperor wanted to know one thing:
What lies on the other side of the ocean?

He sent two hundred ships west into the Atlantic.
Only one came back.
Its captain spoke of a current in the open sea, like a river, that carried the others away.

So the king built two thousand ships.
He left a deputy to rule.
He sailed west.
And he never came back.

That deputy was Mansa Musa.
Years later, Musa told the story in Cairo, and a historian wrote it down.

No wreck. No proof. Just his words.

But nearly two hundred years before Columbus, an African king gave up a throne built on gold.
Not for more gold.
For a question.

## Facts corrected from the draft
- Mansa Musa was not his brother; Musa called him only "my predecessor" and never named him. "Abu Bakr II" is a later attribution that historians question (some suggest Mansa Muhammad ibn Qu).
- Single source: Musa's account in Cairo (1324), recorded by al-Umari. First fleet: 200 ships of men plus 200 of gold, water and provisions; one returned reporting a "river" current in the open sea. Second: 2,000 ships (1,000 men, 1,000 supplies); Musa left as deputy.
- Some historians suspect Musa invented or shaped the story to explain how he took power.
- "Richest empire on Earth" softened to "richest gold fields"; "1311 / 181 years" softened to "nearly two hundred years before Columbus" (the date is inferred).
- "Richest man who ever lived" dropped (unverifiable).
- Cut: Columbus "black men with gold-tipped spears" claim. It is from his 1498 (third) voyage via Las Casas, not 1492, is second-hand and was not verified this session.
- Cairo gold-price fall (reported by al-Umari) written from memory; verify.
